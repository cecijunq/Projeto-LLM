[2410.19775v1] Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4 and BERT, have rapidly gained traction in natural language processing (NLP) and are now integral to financial decision-making. However, their deployment introduces critical challenges, particularly in perpetuating gender biases that can distort decision-making outcomes in high-stakes economic environments. This paper investigates gender bias in LLMs through both mathematical proofs and empirical experiments using the Word Embedding Association Test (WEAT), demonstrating that LLMs inherently reinforce gender stereotypes even without explicit gender markers. By comparing the decision-making processes of humans and LLMs, we reveal fundamental differences: while humans can override biases through ethical reasoning and individualized understanding, LLMs lack such capabilities, leading to biased outputs. The findings highlight the need for developing bias-aware algorithms and ethical frameworks to mitigate the adverse impacts of gender bias in LLMs.
[2406.13677v3] This paper focuses on gender representation bias in the training corpora of large language models (LLMs), which is an underexplored area compared to gender stereotyping bias. The authors propose a novel LLM-based method to detect and quantify gender representation bias in gendered language corpora. They argue that imbalances in the training data, such as unequal frequency of references to individuals of different genders, constitute an upstream source of bias that can propagate and intensify throughout the model lifecycle. The study highlights the importance of addressing gender representation bias to mitigate the downstream effects of gender bias in LLMs. The results demonstrate the feasibility of using LLMs to measure such biases and emphasize the need for more balanced training data to reduce biased outputs in downstream tasks.

[2207.10245v1] This paper investigates how language models become biased during their training process, focusing on the evolution of gender bias in an English language model. Using a relatively small language model with LSTM architecture trained on an English Wikipedia corpus, the authors analyze how gender representation develops over time. They find that gender representation is dynamic and identify different phases during training that contribute to the emergence of bias. The study provides insights into the internal mechanisms of bias formation and highlights the importance of understanding the root causes of gender bias in language models to develop effective mitigation strategies. The findings suggest that gender information is encoded in the model's internal state and that this encodement influences downstream tasks such as semantic textual similarity.
